# Human Nonverbal Communication and Signed-Language Evidence

Status: `PASS_2_EVIDENCE_REVIEW / HUMAN_SCOPE_ONLY`

Predecessor checkpoint: `HUMAN_VERBAL_PASS_1@ba5e8f14cd01a41e1b096fb653d2df120e3d1f34`

This pass asks what happens to UNVTRSLR's semantic-bootstrap thesis when acoustic speech is removed. It deliberately separates **signed languages**, **emerging/homesign systems**, **silent gesture/pantomime**, and **nonlinguistic human signals**. Collapsing these into one category called `nonverbal language` would erase the very evidence the project needs.

Evidence labels:

- `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK` — directly supported by cited research;
- `SUPPORTED_LIMITED_SCOPE` — supported under narrower human conditions;
- `PROJECT_HYPOTHESIS` — UNVTRSLR proposal motivated but not established by the literature;
- `INFERENCE` — synthesis across findings;
- `CHALLENGE` — adversarial implication or false-success risk;
- `UNKNOWN` — evidence does not settle the issue.

## 1. Signed languages are languages, not elaborated mime

Established signed languages exhibit phonological organization, morphology, syntax, discourse structure, prosody, reference, productivity, and conventionalized community norms. Their modality changes what forms are easy to express and how information can be layered, but does not reduce them to a collection of intuitive gestures.

Brentari & Goldin-Meadow's language-emergence review explicitly contrasts established signed languages with homesign and cospeech gesture and treats established signed languages as complex linguistic systems. Loos, German & Meier review the extensive simultaneous structure available through multiple manual and nonmanual articulators.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN LANGUAGE`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** a signed-language control must ingest the signed signal itself — video, tracked articulation, or another modality-preserving representation — rather than an English gloss. An English gloss would pre-solve segmentation, lexical identity, simultaneity, reference, and much of the grammar.

**Claim ceiling:** human signed languages still inherit human cognition, embodiment, sociality, perceptual capacities, and historical/community processes. Their success does not show that a visual-gestural grammar is species-neutral.

References:
- Brentari, D. & Goldin-Meadow, S. (2017). *Language Emergence*. Annual Review of Linguistics 3:363-388. DOI `10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011415-040743`.
- Loos, C., German, A. & Meier, R. P. (2022). *Simultaneous structures in sign languages: Acquisition and emergence*. Frontiers in Psychology 13:992589. DOI `10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992589`.

## 2. Sequential token streams are not a safe universal abstraction

The visual-gestural modality permits multiple articulators to encode linguistic information at the same time. Signed languages can coordinate two hands plus face, head, torso, spatial loci, and timing; simultaneous information can participate in phonology, inflection, spatial relations, discourse, and information structure. Sign languages also use sequential organization, so the contrast is quantitative/structural rather than absolute.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** the strongest ingress representation cannot assume that a message is a one-dimensional sequence of discrete tokens. Candidate segmentation should permit overlapping temporal spans, independently moving articulators, and features whose relation is synchrony rather than adjacency.

**False-success risk:** converting signing into a sequence of gloss tokens before the learner sees it creates a hidden common language and destroys the modality challenge.

## 3. Spatial structure can become grammar — but physical space is not automatically shared semantics

Senghas & Coppola (2001) documented the increasing systematic use of spatial modulation across cohorts of Nicaraguan Sign Language (NSL). Senghas, Kita & Ozyürek (2004) showed that later cohorts increasingly segmented motion events into discrete elements rather than preserving the holistic co-speech gestures of the surrounding hearing population.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / EMERGING HUMAN LANGUAGE`

**What this supports:** a community can conventionalize and restructure visually grounded spatial material into linguistic organization over relatively short historical time.

**What it does not support:** that allocentric/egocentric axes, object boundaries, perspective, spatial loci, or geometric categories are universal semantic primitives. Those are still potential shared-world subsidies.

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** spatial grounding tests need renderer/frame permutations and asymmetric-perspective controls. The learner should earn correspondences across frames rather than receive a common coordinate system.

References:
- Senghas, A. & Coppola, M. (2001). *Children Creating Language: How Nicaraguan Sign Language Acquired a Spatial Grammar*. Psychological Science 12(4):323-328. DOI `10.1111/1467-9280.00359`.
- Senghas, A., Kita, S. & Ozyürek, A. (2004). *Children creating core properties of language: evidence from an emerging sign language in Nicaragua*. Science 305(5691):1779-1782. DOI `10.1126/science.1100199`.

## 4. Community and transmission materially change what emerges

Brentari & Goldin-Meadow distinguish homesigners, early NSL cohorts, and later cohorts to isolate the effects of a system bearing the full communicative burden, participation in a linguistic community, and receiving a language model. Their synthesis argues that homesign acquires many language-like properties individually but that community use and transmission support additional structure characteristic of a full language.

NSL's sequential cohorts systematized aspects of the emerging language. Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language (ABSL) likewise developed systematic grammatical structure in a community, with later work showing gradual increases in prosodic and syntactic complexity.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN LANGUAGE EMERGENCE`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** a two-agent bootstrap and a community language are different experimental regimes. A dyad may establish a useful bridge without exhibiting structures that depend on repeated community transmission.

**False-success risk:** requiring mature language-like grammar as the acceptance bar could incorrectly fail a successful semantic bridge; conversely, a dyad's private convention should not be called a language merely because it coordinates well.

References:
- Sandler, W., Meir, I., Padden, C. & Aronoff, M. (2005). *The emergence of grammar: Systematic structure in a new language*. PNAS 102(7):2661-2665. DOI `10.1073/pnas.0405448102`.
- Sandler, W., Meir, I., Dachkovsky, S., Padden, C. & Aronoff, M. (2011). *The emergence of complexity in prosody and syntax*. Lingua 121(13):2014-2033. DOI `10.1016/j.lingua.2011.05.007`.

## 5. Homesign demonstrates spontaneous structure, but the evidence must be handled with care

Foundational homesign research studies deaf individuals who lacked sustained access to a conventional spoken or signed language and created gesture systems used as their primary communication. Studies report stable forms, combinatorial/hierarchical organization, reference, displacement, and other language-like properties that exceed the structure of surrounding hearing caregivers' cospeech gesture.

Carrigan & Coppola (2017) found that adult homesign structure cannot be explained simply by successful communicative problem solving with family members; relatives often comprehended homesign poorly even though the systems were structured.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN DEVELOPMENT`

**Important contemporary caveat:** Goico & Horton (2023) review contested terminology, methods, theoretical assumptions, and Deaf-studies critiques in homesign scholarship. UNVTRSLR should not treat early generative interpretations as uncontested facts about what counts as `language`, nor portray deaf people merely as natural experiments.

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** homesign supports the possibility that humans impose substantial structure on a communication system without a conventional language model. It does **not** prove that successful communication itself causes that structure, and it does not identify which biases would generalize beyond humans.

References:
- Carrigan, E. M. & Coppola, M. (2017). *Successful communication does not drive language development: Evidence from adult homesign*. Cognition 158:10-27. DOI `10.1016/j.cognition.2016.09.012`.
- Goico, S. A. & Horton, L. (2023). *Homesign: Contested Issues*. Annual Review of Linguistics 9:377-398. DOI `10.1146/annurev-linguistics-030521-060001`.

## 6. Silent gesture can reorganize when it bears the full communicative burden

Goldin-Meadow and colleagues found that hearing speakers asked to communicate events without speech produce gesture structures that differ from their ordinary cospeech gestures. Goldin-Meadow, So, Ozyürek & Mylander (2008) found a common actor-patient-act ordering across speakers of several languages in nonverbal event-representation tasks, despite different dominant spoken word orders.

Laboratory conventionalization work by Verhoef et al. showed that repeated silent-gesture coordination can become more efficient and conventionalized over interaction.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN EXPERIMENT`

**Claim ceiling:** these results are often interpreted as revealing human cognitive/communicative biases. They should not be promoted into a universal event grammar for arbitrary minds.

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** use silent gesture as a bridge condition between full natural language and arbitrary synthetic channels. Compare one-shot pantomime with repeated dyadic conventionalization and with a community/transmission condition.

References:
- Goldin-Meadow, S., So, W. C., Ozyürek, A. & Mylander, C. (2008). *The natural order of events: how speakers of different languages represent events nonverbally*. PNAS 105(27):9163-9168. DOI `10.1073/pnas.0710060105`.
- Verhoef, T., et al. (2016). *Measuring conventionalization in the manual modality*. Journal of Language Evolution 1(2):109-118. DOI `10.1093/jole/lzw005`.

## 7. Gesture and speech are often an integrated multimodal system

Kendon and Goldin-Meadow review evidence that gestures accompanying speech can add information not present in speech and are integrated with discourse/cognition. Cospeech gesture is therefore neither meaningless noise nor equivalent to an autonomous signed language.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** a signalhood detector must allow meaning-bearing information to be distributed across channels. The relevant unit may be a multimodal temporal construction rather than a token in one stream.

**False-success risk:** transcribing speech while discarding gesture, or conversely treating every gesture as an independent linguistic symbol, changes the evidence available to the learner.

References:
- Kendon, A. (1997). *Gesture*. Annual Review of Anthropology 26:109-128. DOI `10.1146/annurev.anthro.26.1.109`.
- Goldin-Meadow, S. (1999). *The role of gesture in communication and thinking*. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 3(11):419-429. DOI `10.1016/S1364-6613(99)01397-2`.

## 8. Pointing is not a universal dictionary entry for `that`

Human infants can use pointing in social-attentional interaction, and pointing is a powerful human deictic resource. But a pointing form is not semantically self-interpreting. Its function depends on geometry, shared scene assumptions, participant roles, salience, gaze, and pragmatic context.

Sign-language evidence makes the boundary sharper: Cormier, Schembri & Woll (2013) argue that pronominal pointing signs share properties with both linguistic pronouns and non-signer pointing gestures and cannot be characterized exclusively as one or the other.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN COMMUNICATION`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** `pointing` is an observed form/trajectory, not an evaluator-provided semantic primitive. The bootstrap must infer whether a point functions as reference, direction, command, location, participant marking, discourse management, or something else in context.

References:
- Liszkowski, U., Carpenter, M., Henning, A., Striano, T. & Tomasello, M. (2004). *Twelve-month-olds point to share attention and interest*. Developmental Science 7(3):297-307. DOI `10.1111/j.1467-7687.2004.00349.x`.
- Cormier, K., Schembri, A. & Woll, B. (2013). *Pronouns and pointing in sign languages*. Lingua 137:230-247. DOI `10.1016/j.lingua.2013.09.010`.

## 9. Iconicity is pervasive in the manual modality but still not an answer key

Perniss, Thompson & Vigliocco review iconicity across spoken and signed languages and argue that motivated form-meaning mappings are widespread. Sign languages provide abundant opportunities for iconic structure because movement and spatial form can resemble aspects of referents/events.

Yet conventional signs remain language-specific, can become historically opaque, and are embedded in grammar. Iconicity does not tell a naïve receiver which dimension of resemblance is intended.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / HUMAN LANGUAGE`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** visual iconicity should be treated like vocal iconicity in Pass 1: a graded accelerator whose availability and interpretation are part of the condition. Include false-iconicity controls where a salient resemblance tracks the wrong factor.

Reference: Perniss, P., Thompson, R. L. & Vigliocco, G. (2010). *Iconicity as a General Property of Language: Evidence from Spoken and Signed Languages*. Frontiers in Psychology 1:227. DOI `10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00227`.

## 10. Nonmanual signals can be linguistic, paralinguistic, affective, or incidental

Signed languages recruit face, head, torso, gaze, and timing for grammatical/prosodic functions as well as discourse and affective functions. Emerging ABSL work shows facial and rhythmic cues becoming more systematically aligned with grammatical constituency over time.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** the same physical channel can carry multiple functional layers. A face movement should not be preclassified as `emotion`, `grammar`, `attention`, or `noise` before context/contingency evidence supports that interpretation.

## 11. Facial-expression universality is not safe bootstrap canon

Gendron, Crivelli & Barrett (2018) review cross-cultural work using more discovery-oriented methods and argue that how perceivers make meaning of facial movements shows substantial diversity, challenging simple universal one-expression/one-emotion mappings.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_PRIOR_WORK / CONTESTED FIELD`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** do not preload `smile = happy`, `wide eyes = fear`, or similar mappings. Facial movements may be useful signals, but their function has to be learned in context and may differ culturally or interactionally.

**Control requirement:** include a facial-expression trap where an evaluator's human category label conflicts with the partner's actual functional use.

Reference: Gendron, M., Crivelli, C. & Barrett, L. F. (2018). *Universality Reconsidered: Diversity in Making Meaning of Facial Expressions*. Current Directions in Psychological Science 27(4):211-219. DOI `10.1177/0963721417746794`.

## 12. Touch and interpersonal distance are communication surfaces, not universal semantics

A recent integrative review identifies multiple behavioral/communicative functions for human touch and emphasizes the versatility of tactile interaction. Touch can carry social, regulatory, affiliative, warning, or instrumental functions depending on context.

**Evidence class:** `SUPPORTED_LIMITED_SCOPE / REVIEW`

**UNVTRSLR consequence:** tactile channels should be allowed by the architecture, but no stable touch-to-meaning code should be assumed. Proxemic distance likewise belongs in the observable context rather than in a universal lexicon unless independently grounded.

Reference: Saluja, S., Croy, I. & Stevenson, R. J. (2024). *The Functions of Human Touch: An Integrative Review*. Journal of Nonverbal Behavior 48:387-417. DOI `10.1007/s10919-024-00464-x`.

## 13. Strongest challenge to the Pass 1 bootstrap from this evidence

The Pass 1 framing still risks being too **serial, dyadic, and object-centered**.

Signed-language evidence adds at least four pressures:

1. information may be simultaneous across independent articulators;
2. spatial loci can become linguistic resources, making `environment` and `channel` partly interpenetrating;
3. a community can reshape a system beyond what a single dyad creates;
4. observed forms like points, facial actions, or movements do not have fixed functional class before conventional/contextual evidence.

Therefore `message`, `referent`, `turn`, and `channel` should remain hypotheses where the strongest condition does not provide them.

## 14. Implications for R0.5 identifiability and attribution

Pass 2 strengthens Thirteen's T1/T2 split.

A manual or facial cue can be **informationally discriminating** while its functional attribution remains unresolved:

- a point may identify a locus without establishing whether it is pronoun, demonstrative, direction, or command;
- a facial movement may segment grammar, express affect, manage interaction, or be incidental;
- spatial regularity may come from a shared display coordinate system supplied by the experiment rather than from the participants' bridge.

So R0.5 should preserve at least:

- availability of a distinguishing witness;
- provenance of that witness;
- functional-class uncertainty;
- whether the witness depends on shared embodiment/geometry;
- whether the structure was pre-existing, negotiated in a dyad, or stabilized through community transmission.

## 15. First-100 consequences

The existing 100 challenge labels should remain evaluator descriptions. Pass 2 changes the dependency assumptions:

- meta-communication may be simultaneous with referential content rather than a later token;
- participant reference may emerge from spatial/deictic practice rather than from a dedicated `self/other` symbol;
- composition need not be sequential concatenation;
- a shared spatial frame may itself need grounding;
- community/transmission effects should be tested separately from dyadic bootstrap;
- object-based challenges require a control where another segmentation is equally viable;
- `attention-getting` should not be equated with gaze or pointing.

## 16. Current claim ceiling after Pass 2 research

Human manual/nonverbal evidence materially supports:

- full linguistic organization without speech;
- spontaneous structured communication under severely reduced conventional linguistic input;
- rapid community-driven conventionalization and grammatical emergence;
- simultaneous/multichannel linguistic encoding;
- deictic and iconic routes that can aid grounding;
- multimodal integration and nonlinguistic communication through gesture, face, and touch.

It does **not** establish:

- visual gesture as a universal bootstrap medium;
- a universal event order;
- universal pointing semantics;
- universal facial-expression meanings;
- universal spatial frames;
- universal iconicity;
- that a dyadic convention will become a language;
- or that language-like structure is necessary for a successful semantic bridge.

Those remain project hypotheses, control conditions, or open questions.
