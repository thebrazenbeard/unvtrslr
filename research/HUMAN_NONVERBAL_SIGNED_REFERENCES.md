## Signed-language emergence, homesign, and community effects

### Brentari, D. & Goldin-Meadow, S. (2017). “Language Emergence.” *Annual Review of Linguistics* 3, 363-388.

DOI: `10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011415-040743`

**Use:** compares established signed languages, homesign, and emerging sign systems to ask which linguistic properties arise in individuals, communities, and transmitted language. Supports category separation, not a universal-grammar claim.

### Senghas, A. & Coppola, M. (2001). “Children Creating Language: How Nicaraguan Sign Language Acquired a Spatial Grammar.” *Psychological Science* 12(4), 323-328.

DOI: `10.1111/1467-9280.00359`

**Use:** evidence that systematic spatial modulation increased across NSL cohorts. Supports community/cohort conventionalization and spatial-grammar controls.

### Senghas, A., Kita, S. & Ozyürek, A. (2004). “Children creating core properties of language: evidence from an emerging sign language in Nicaragua.” *Science* 305(5691), 1779-1782.

DOI: `10.1126/science.1100199`

**Use:** later NSL cohorts increasingly segmented motion events rather than preserving surrounding hearing speakers' holistic co-speech gestures. Supports transmission/systematization, with human-specific claim ceiling.

### Sandler, W., Meir, I., Padden, C. & Aronoff, M. (2005). “The emergence of grammar: Systematic structure in a new language.” *PNAS* 102(7), 2661-2665.

DOI: `10.1073/pnas.0405448102`

**Use:** Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language evidence for systematic grammatical structure in a young community language.

### Sandler, W., Meir, I., Dachkovsky, S., Padden, C. & Aronoff, M. (2011). “The emergence of complexity in prosody and syntax.” *Lingua* 121(13), 2014-2033.

DOI: `10.1016/j.lingua.2011.05.007`

**Use:** evidence for increasing prosodic/syntactic complexity in an emerging sign language; supports separating early bridge utility from later language complexity.

### Carrigan, E. M. & Coppola, M. (2017). “Successful communication does not drive language development: Evidence from adult homesign.” *Cognition* 158, 10-27.

DOI: `10.1016/j.cognition.2016.09.012`

**Use:** structured adult homesign cannot be explained simply by successful family communication. Supports caution against deriving linguistic structure from task success alone.

### Goico, S. A. & Horton, L. (2023). “Homesign: Contested Issues.” *Annual Review of Linguistics* 9, 377-398.

DOI: `10.1146/annurev-linguistics-030521-060001`

**Use:** reviews methodological, terminological, theoretical, and Deaf-studies disputes around homesign research. Required counterweight to treating foundational homesign interpretations as uncontested.

## Simultaneity, multimodality, gesture, and pointing

### Loos, C., German, A. & Meier, R. P. (2022). “Simultaneous structures in sign languages: Acquisition and emergence.” *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:992589.

DOI: `10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992589`

**Use:** reviews multiple simultaneous structures supported by manual and nonmanual articulators. Direct pressure against one-dimensional sequential-token ingress.

### Kendon, A. (1997). “Gesture.” *Annual Review of Anthropology* 26, 109-128.

DOI: `10.1146/annurev.anthro.26.1.109`

**Use:** broad review of gesture as an organized communicative phenomenon integrated with interaction rather than a simple collection of lexical signs.

### Goldin-Meadow, S. (1999). “The role of gesture in communication and thinking.” *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 3(11), 419-429.

DOI: `10.1016/S1364-6613(99)01397-2`

**Use:** evidence that gesture can contribute information and interact with speech/thought; motivates multimodal rather than single-channel analysis.

### Goldin-Meadow, S., So, W. C., Ozyürek, A. & Mylander, C. (2008). “The natural order of events: how speakers of different languages represent events nonverbally.” *PNAS* 105(27), 9163-9168.

DOI: `10.1073/pnas.0710060105`

**Use:** cross-linguistic silent-gesture ordering regularities. Useful as evidence of human event-representation biases, not a species-neutral universal grammar.

### Verhoef, T., et al. (2016). “Measuring conventionalization in the manual modality.” *Journal of Language Evolution* 1(2), 109-118.

DOI: `10.1093/jole/lzw005`

**Use:** experimental evidence for manual-signal conventionalization over repeated interaction.

### Liszkowski, U., Carpenter, M., Henning, A., Striano, T. & Tomasello, M. (2004). “Twelve-month-olds point to share attention and interest.” *Developmental Science* 7(3), 297-307.

DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-7687.2004.00349.x`

**Use:** human developmental evidence for pointing in shared-attention interaction. Does not establish a universal `point = reference` mapping.

### Cormier, K., Schembri, A. & Woll, B. (2013). “Pronouns and pointing in sign languages.” *Lingua* 137, 230-247.

DOI: `10.1016/j.lingua.2013.09.010`

**Use:** pointing signs share properties with linguistic pronouns and non-signer pointing. Supports treating physical form and communicative function as separable hypotheses.

## Iconicity and nonmanual/facial behavior

### Perniss, P., Thompson, R. L. & Vigliocco, G. (2010). “Iconicity as a General Property of Language: Evidence from Spoken and Signed Languages.” *Frontiers in Psychology* 1:227.

DOI: `10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00227`

**Use:** reviews iconicity across spoken and signed modalities. Supports iconicity as a graded human condition, not as an automatic semantic decoder.

### Gendron, M., Crivelli, C. & Barrett, L. F. (2018). “Universality Reconsidered: Diversity in Making Meaning of Facial Expressions.” *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 27(4), 211-219.

DOI: `10.1177/0963721417746794`

**Use:** reviews cross-cultural variability in interpretation of facial movements and challenges simple one-expression/one-emotion universality claims. The broader field remains contested, so the project uses this as a strong warning against hard-coded facial semantics rather than a claim that no cross-cultural regularities exist.

## Touch

### Saluja, S., Croy, I. & Stevenson, R. J. (2024). “The Functions of Human Touch: An Integrative Review.” *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* 48, 387-417.

DOI: `10.1007/s10919-024-00464-x`

**Use:** reviews the diverse behavioral and communicative functions of human touch. Supports treating touch as an available channel whose meaning remains context-dependent rather than a universal code.

## Evidence discipline

These sources collectively support human modality diversity, spontaneous structure, conventionalization, multimodal signaling, and community effects. They do not demonstrate species-neutral visual semantics, universal pointing, universal facial expressions, universal event order, or a universal spatial grammar. Those remain controls, project hypotheses, or open questions.
